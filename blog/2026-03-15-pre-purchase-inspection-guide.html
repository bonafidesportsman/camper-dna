What to look for when inspecting a VW T5 or T6 before you buy

A campervan is not a car. The conversion adds complexity — and problems — that a standard vehicle check won't catch. Here's what to look for before you hand over any money.

A campervan is a significant purchase. Fifteen minutes of proper inspection is worth considerably more than the price of a second trip, a specialist inspection, or — worst case — a repair bill after you've already bought it. I've learned this the hard way more than once.

This guide takes you through a methodical inspection process. Print it or open it on your phone before every viewing. Go through it section by section. And if a seller objects to you spending time checking the van properly, that tells you something important: keep looking.

Start before you arrive: the paperwork check

Some of the most important inspection work happens before you ever see the van. A vehicle check costs about £5–10 and takes five minutes online. It's the difference between buying a bargain and buying someone else's nightmare.

What to check online:

  • Check the registered keeper history on a paid vehicle check (HPI, Cazana or similar). Confirm outstanding finance, write-off history, stolen status, and plate changes. If the van has had more than three keepers, ask why.
  • Verify the MOT history at gov.uk/check-mot-history. Note any recurring advisories — multiple advisories about the same component suggest that component is failing.
  • Ask the seller who did the conversion, when, and whether they have documentation. This matters more than people realise.
  • Ask for photos of the conversion before viewing if you haven't already seen them. If the seller won't provide photos, walk away.

Finally, check the service history. A T5 or T6 with gaps in servicing has lived hard. A van that's been serviced every 10,000 miles at a VW dealer or trusted independent is worth the premium over one with questionable paperwork.

The base vehicle: working around systematically

The conversion is the sexy part, but the base vehicle matters more. A failed engine or gearbox will cost you thousands. A poor-quality conversion will annoy you but won't strand you on the motorway. Work around the van methodically, starting with the exterior. Don't get distracted by the living space before you've checked the vehicle itself.

Bodywork and chassis

Start with the metal underneath. Rust is the silent killer on T5s especially — sills and wheel arches are where water gets trapped and corrosion starts.

  • Panel gaps should be consistent all round. Inconsistency suggests accident repair, which means someone else's insurance claim and potentially structural issues.
  • Paint should match consistently across the van. Check door edges and shut lines closely — respray signs here are common on accident repairs.
  • Examine the sills and wheel arches carefully. On T5s over 100,000 miles, surface rust is normal; structural rust is a no-go.
  • Look underneath: check the chassis rails and floor pan for excessive corrosion. Use your phone torch and lie down if you need to. Sellers rarely check here.
  • All lights should work — front, rear, indicators, fog lights. A broken light isn't a deal-breaker but suggests the van hasn't been well-maintained.
  • Check the windscreen for chips or cracks. These are MOT advisories waiting to happen, and stone chips get worse on motorway drives.
  • Inspect the tyres: tread depth should be even across each tyre (uneven wear suggests suspension issues), no cracking on sidewalls, and check the DOT date stamp. Tyres older than five years should be replaced.
  • Confirm a spare wheel is present and in usable condition. You'd be surprised how many are missing or dry-rotted.

Pop-top or high-roof (if fitted)

If the van has a pop-top roof, inspect it carefully. This is a common failure point because canvas and seals take a beating from the weather.

  • The canvas should be in good condition with no tears or holes. Check the seams — if they're coming apart, repairs are expensive.
  • The mechanism should operate smoothly with no sticking or grinding noises.
  • When closed, it should close flush with no visible gaps around the perimeter.
  • The struts should hold the roof up without assistance. If you have to support it, they've lost pressure and need replacement.
  • Check for visible seals around the perimeter. If seals are cracked or missing, water ingress is inevitable.

The engine: what matters

If you're not mechanically confident, bring someone who is with you, or pay for an independent specialist inspection. A mechanic will cost £100–£150 to put the van on a ramp and check the things you can't see. On a purchase of this size, it's money well spent.

At minimum, do this yourself:

  • Check the oil level and condition. Dark black oil suggests an overdue service. Grey or milky oil suggests coolant contamination — a serious problem indicating head gasket failure.
  • Check the coolant level and colour. It should be green or blue, not brown. Brown coolant means the system hasn't been flushed properly.
  • Look for visible oil leaks around the engine and gearbox. Small weeps are normal on older engines; major leaks are not.
  • Inspect the drive belts. They should be intact with no cracking. Cracked belts fail without warning.
  • Check that battery terminals are clean and tight. Corroded terminals suggest poor electrical maintenance.
  • Verify the service history is present and consistent with mileage. A van serviced every 10,000 miles at a dealer is far better than one with gaps.

Engine types: Both T5 and T6 typically use 2.0-litre TDI diesels. The T6 added a BiTDI variant (190 or 204 PS) which offers better pulling power. All are proven engines with hundreds of thousands of miles across the user community. Turbo servicing is important — cheap oil will destroy these engines.

Take it for a drive — the real test

A proper test drive is non-negotiable. Not just up the road — take it for 15–20 minutes at various speeds and over different road surfaces. This is where mechanical problems become obvious.

  • Cold start: Does the engine fire immediately and idle smoothly? Poor cold starts or rough idling suggest fuel system or injector issues.
  • Dashboard warning lights: There should be none when the engine is running. An orange check engine light means diagnostics are needed before you buy.
  • Gearbox: Manual or DSG, changes should be smooth through all gears. Listen for grinding or hesitation. DSG boxes sometimes shudder between gears on older T5s — this is a known wear issue.
  • Clutch (manual only): Should engage smoothly without juddering. Slipping suggests wear.
  • Brakes: Progressive and even, with no pulling to one side. Brake fade during repeated application suggests the system needs attention.
  • Steering: No vibration at speed or pulling to one side. Vibration often indicates suspension wear.
  • Unusual noises: Listen under acceleration, braking, and over bumps. Metallic knocking under load suggests engine problems. Clunking over bumps suggests suspension wear.
  • Turbo: The turbo should spool up cleanly. A slight whistle is normal; a loud whine or lag suggests boost problems.

Drive the van as a buyer would use it. If it's heavy with conversion weight, notice how it handles. If there are conversion-specific features (gas heater, for example), test them while you're moving to see if they work under way.

The conversion: quality and condition

Now we get to the living space. A poor conversion can be lived with; water ingress cannot. Look for three things: build quality, evidence of damp, and whether the systems work.

General joinery and finish

  • All joinery should be secure with no movement or rattling. Open drawers and lockers and close them again. Everything should operate smoothly.
  • Surfaces should be consistent with no gaps between units and walls larger than 5mm. Large gaps suggest poor installation or settling after conversion.
  • The worktop should be in good condition with no swelling, delamination, or water damage at the edges. Damage here means water has been inside the cabinetry.
  • Overhead lockers should be secure and closing properly. These take a beating in transit.
  • Floor covering should be in good condition with no lifting or water damage underneath. Lift a corner if you can and check for damp.

The smell test

This matters more than any checklist. Open the van, stand inside for a minute, and notice. A fresh, dry smell is good. A musty or damp smell is a red flag — somewhere water is getting in, and you won't know where until you've owned it for a few months. A diesel smell inside (other than immediately after a heater run) is worth investigating — it suggests a fuel leak or heating system problem.

No amount of air freshener covers real damp. Walk away if something smells wrong.

Electrical system: more important than people think

The electrical system is what makes a campervan a home rather than a van. A failed leisure battery or broken solar panels will ruin your trip. Test everything.

  • A leisure battery should be fitted. Ask what type and capacity — AGM or lithium (LiFePO4), 100Ah or larger is standard. Check the age; AGM batteries have a 3–5 year lifespan.
  • All 12v sockets and USB ports should work. Carry a phone charger and test at least two sockets.
  • All lights should function — interior, reading lights, exterior lights, everything.
  • If solar panels are fitted, check for cracks and visible damage. Ask the seller if they can show you the controller output. Solar panels in good condition should output 5–10 amps per 100W of panel in decent daylight.
  • If a 240v hookup is fitted, test it with a campsite cable or ask the seller for a demonstration. Faulty hookup wiring is dangerous.
  • If an inverter is fitted (converts 12v to 240v), confirm it actually powers 240v loads. Some are underpowered for their rating.
  • Inspect the wiring: look for exposed cables, non-standard connections, or signs of unsafe DIY electrical work. Wire nuts instead of proper connectors are a red flag.
  • Check that a split charge system or DC-DC charger is fitted. Start the engine and watch the leisure battery voltage rise on the display — if it doesn't, the charger isn't working.

Red flags: Fuses taped over instead of replaced. Multiple leads running from the vehicle battery instead of through a proper split-charge system. Exposed battery terminals with no proper covers. Any of these suggest the electrical system has been installed by someone who didn't know what they were doing.

Water system: test it now, not later

A failed water system means cold showers and no tap. Test everything.

  • Note the fresh water tank location and capacity. 20–40 litres is typical for basic spec; larger tanks are better if you boondock.
  • Test the water pump: fill the tank and turn on a tap. Water should flow. If it's weak, the pump might be failing or the tank might be partly blocked.
  • Check for water leaks around fittings, the pump, and tank connections. Even small leaks will drip onto your bed or electrics.
  • Test the drain tap. It should be accessible and turn fully without binding.
  • Check that the waste pipe drains properly. Ask the seller where it goes — if it just drains onto the ground, you'll need to manage that when parked.
  • If a shower is fitted, test it fully — hot water, cold water, drain flow, and seals. Showers leak if they're not maintained properly.

Heating: essential in the UK

A night heater is the difference between comfortable camping and a miserable night. UK campervans without heating are incomplete.

  • A diesel night heater should be fitted if specified. Test it during the viewing if possible — ask the seller to turn it on and listen for clean operation.
  • Branded heaters (Webasto or Eberspacher) are preferred. Once warm, they should be nearly silent. A smell of smoke or diesel suggests a fault. Confirm that fuel is being drawn from the van tank, not a separate canister.
  • Non-branded diesel heaters are cheaper but need careful inspection. Test fully, confirm fuel supply is set up properly, and check for error codes on the controller.
  • Ducting should be secure and routed sensibly. Loose ducting means heat escapes.
  • A CO (carbon monoxide) detector should be fitted and working. Test the alarm.
  • A smoke detector should also be present.
  • If a gas system is fitted, check the hose condition and connections. The regulator should have a visible date stamp within 10 years. Gas lockers must vent to outside. Test that gas appliances light cleanly with no hesitation or smell.

Important: Gas in a vehicle is a specialist area. An unsound installation is genuinely dangerous. If a gas hob or fridge is fitted, ask when the installation was last checked by a qualified gas installer. Cheap gas installations are a false economy.

Sleeping: comfort and condition

You'll spend a third of your time in bed. Make sure it works.

  • The bed should lower or fold flat easily. No mechanical parts should require force.
  • Test the sleeping surface yourself. Lie on it and check that it's comfortable and long enough. If you're tall, measure the bed — some conversions skimp on length.
  • Check the mattress or platform for condition and damp underneath. Damp under the bed is common if water ingress has been an issue.
  • If it's a rock and roll bed, the mechanism should be smooth. All catches should work. The back should lock securely in the upright position.
  • If it's a fixed bed, check that storage access underneath is clear and functional.

Seating and safety

This section is often overlooked but it matters legally and practically.

  • Any rear seats fitted should have functioning seatbelts. Test them.
  • Seat fixings should be bolted to the vehicle floor or a proper load-rated mounting system — not just bolted to the conversion floor. This is a safety issue if you ever need to brake hard.
  • A fire extinguisher should be fitted and within its service date.
  • A first aid kit should be present.
  • If a roof ventilation fan is fitted, test that it works. Ventilation is essential in the UK climate.

Documents: request these before you buy

If the seller can't produce these, don't buy. Period.

  • V5C (logbook) — check that the registered keeper details match the seller. If they don't, ask why.
  • Full service history — ideally stamped by a VW dealer or trusted independent.
  • Conversion documentation — spec sheet, receipt, contact details for the conversion company.
  • Warranty documentation for the conversion or specialist equipment.
  • Receipt for any significant recent work (roof repair, new bed, electrical upgrade, etc.).
  • MOT certificate with no major advisories.

After the inspection

If everything checks out and you're considering making an offer, consider paying for a specialist pre-purchase inspection. An independent VW specialist can put the vehicle on a ramp and check items that aren't visible during a viewing — suspension, exhaust, brakes, hidden corrosion. Many will do this for £100–£150 and it's money well spent on a purchase of this size.

A campervan will take you places and hold memories. Buy the right one and you'll still be happy in five years. Rush the inspection and you'll spend that time annoyed with yourself.
