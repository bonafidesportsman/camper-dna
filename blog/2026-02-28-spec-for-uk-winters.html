Year-round van life: how to spec your VW for UK winters

A summer van is easy. A van that's genuinely comfortable in February in Scotland requires deliberate choices at every stage — here's what they are.

I spent last February in a van on the Moray Firth with the temperature hitting minus 8 at night. The van stayed at 16°C inside without draining the batteries. That wasn't luck. It was spec. And it wasn't cheap.

Year-round van life in the UK is entirely achievable. But it requires a fundamentally different approach to conversion than a summer setup. You can't bolt on heating and hope for the best. Insulation, heating, battery capacity, water systems, condensation management — they all interlock. Get one wrong and the whole system fails.

This is what I've learned from 18 months of winter testing, and what I'd spec into a van if I were building one for year-round UK use today.

Insulation: you're fighting thermal bridging, not just cold

The insulation conversation usually starts with "what thickness?" It should start with "where are the cold spots?"

Your van is a metal box. Metal conducts. Every ceiling rail, floor joist, and panel clip is a thermal bridge — a highway for cold to travel straight through your insulation and into your living space. You can pack 100mm of the best foam into the walls, but if you haven't addressed thermal bridging, you'll still wake up to frost on the windows at 0°C.

This is why experienced winter van builders use closed-cell foam, not open-cell. Closed-cell (typically Kingspan or Celotex, around £60–80 per sheet) creates an actual vapor barrier and doesn't absorb water. Open-cell absorbs moisture and fails in winter conditions. I've seen vans with open-cell foam that literally froze solid inside the insulation layer. Don't do this.

The specification I'd use: 50mm closed-cell foam on walls and floor. 75mm on the roof. This gets you a theoretical U-value around 0.3–0.35, which in practice means your van loses heat slowly enough that a modest heating system can keep up. Cheaper than 100mm, lighter, and far more effective than the cheap polyethylene foam or bubble wrap you see in budget builds.

Then address the thermal bridges. This means: thin rigid foam strips behind every metal rail, phenolic tape around any penetrations, and a thermal break between the floor and the body panels. It's fiddly. It adds maybe £300-400 in materials and two days of work. It also makes a 2°C difference on a freezing night. That matters.

One material that doesn't work: carpet or vinyl on the floor alone. I've seen people think that adding a wool rug or carpet underlay is an alternative to proper insulation. It isn't. A van with carpet and no foam will be miserable in winter — damp, cold, and expensive to heat.

One material that does work if budget is tight: Dodo mat. It's a 25mm sheep's wool automotive mat designed for engine bays. It's not a primary insulation layer, but it's moisture-tolerant, durable, and reasonable value. Use it as a secondary layer under your floor or behind your cladding if you're short on budget or space.

Heating: diesel is the only serious option

Gas heaters are cheaper upfront and simpler to install. They're also dangerous, inefficient in winter, and I wouldn't recommend one for year-round UK use.

Diesel heaters — essentially auxiliary heating systems that tap into your van's fuel tank — are the standard for reason. You get controllable, efficient heat without consuming van power or compromising safety. The two brands you'll see are Webasto (the OEM choice, used by VW factory) and Eberspacher (the premium alternative). There are Chinese clones at half the price.

Webasto Air Top 2000 S: Around £800–1000 installed. Proven, reliable, factory option on many vans. Uses about 0.2 litres of diesel per hour of continuous running. Heating capacity 2kW. It's a mature product with plenty of parts availability and repair knowledge in the community.

Eberspacher Hydronic II D4S: Around £1200–1500 installed. Slightly more efficient, integrated into some newer OEM systems, feels more refined. Same 0.2L/hour consumption. The difference in practice is marginal — you're choosing between two very good systems.

Chinese clone brands (Planar, Autoterm, etc.): £200–400. Some work brilliantly. Some fail after a season. I've seen both. The cost difference is real, but the reliability gamble is bigger. If you're handy with troubleshooting and have a budget constraint, they're worth considering. If you value predictable heating on a freezing night and can stretch to Webasto or Eberspacher, do it.

Running costs: at current diesel prices (roughly 130p/litre), running a 2kW diesel heater for 8 hours a night costs about £2.20. That's genuinely cheap. Compare to running a 2kW electric heater on battery power (which you'd need 400Ah of battery to do for a full night) and the maths flip entirely in diesel's favor.

Thermostatic control is essential. You want a heater that regulates itself — not something you dial up to full blast and then turn off manually. A basic thermostat adds another £100–150 but saves money and battery over the course of a winter.

One last thing: exhaust routing matters. Your diesel heater needs a flue pipe that vents clear of the van, secured properly, with appropriate heat shielding. I've seen installations where the flue was routed too close to the rear bumper or where vibration caused it to loosen mid-journey. Spend the extra time getting this right.

Condensation: the problem nobody talks about until it's too late

Winter condensation is the number-one reason winter van setups fail. It's worse than insufficient heating. It's worse than weak insulation. You can be warm and still be miserable if your van is dripping wet.

Here's why: when you heat a van in winter, the air inside warms up and can hold more moisture. When that warm air hits cold windows or walls, it dumps the moisture instantly. You wake up with condensation running down every window, dripping onto your sleeping bag, settling into your insulation. Keep doing this and your walls start to rot from inside out.

The solution is ventilation combined with active dehumidification. You need to move moist air out of the van faster than heating can create it.

Ventilation fans: A MaxxFan (£150–200 installed) running on a low setting continuously pulls moisture-laden air out and fresher air in. In winter with heating, run it on low speed — you're not trying to cool the van, you're managing moisture. The power draw is minimal (about 20W on low speed) but the effect on condensation is dramatic. Window vents or roof vents add passive circulation, but active extraction is where the real work happens.

Moisture traps: Products like DampRid or silica gel canisters absorb ambient moisture and work without power. They're not a replacement for ventilation, but they're cheap backup. Refresh them regularly (they cost about £4 each and last weeks to months depending on conditions).

Cooking and showering: These are condensation killers. Every time you boil a kettle or take a hot shower, you're pumping litres of water vapor into your van. Open a window immediately. Run the fan on high. Better yet: cook outside, shower at a gym or campsite facility, and keep cooking inside to absolute minimum in winter.

Double-glazed windows help moderately — the inner pane stays warmer than a single-glazed window, so condensation forms less readily. But condensation will still happen on double glazing if you're heating to 18°C and outdoor temperature drops to 0°C. The real protection is ventilation, not window type.

Thermal window covers: SilverScreens or equivalent reflective window covers are underrated. They reduce heat loss through windows by 40–50% and also reduce the temperature differential that triggers condensation. If you're wild camping (not at a site with hook-up) they're worth £50–100 for the set. They don't solve condensation, but they meaningfully reduce it.

Electrical: winter demands are brutal

A summer van with 200W of solar and a 100Ah lithium battery works beautifully. The same setup in January is a nightmare.

Here's what changes: solar output in February is roughly 30–40% of summer output. Meanwhile, your power demands surge. Your diesel heater runs 8 hours a night (not your battery, but your heater pulls parasitic draw). Your lighting runs longer (you wake and sleep with darkness). If you have electric water heating or a small space heater backup, consumption skyrockets.

The honest spec for year-round van life: minimum 200Ah of LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate). Ideally 300Ah if budget allows. This is expensive — a 200Ah LiFePO4 system with integrated BMS, cabling, and charger costs £2500–3500. But it's the difference between having power and having to ration power.

Solar needs to be generous: 600W minimum (two 300W panels), positioned for winter angle (tilted steeper than summer optimal). In winter, 600W gives you 1.5–2kWh on a good day — enough to maintain a battery but not fast. Without a way to charge externally (mains hook-up, DC charging while driving) you'll drain a 200Ah battery in 3–4 days of poor weather.

This is why many serious winter van lifers use mains hook-up at campsites for 50% of winter, then wild camp when power is full and weather is good. It's not defeat — it's sensible system design. A van spec'd to wild camp indefinitely in winter is a van spec'd to an incredibly expensive ceiling.

Charging while driving matters. A 120A DC-DC charger (£800–1200 installed) keeps your battery topped during daily driving. If you're doing 200+ miles per week, you can maintain a 200Ah battery through alternator charging alone. Below that, you'll need external charging or reduced consumption.

Water systems: freezing is the enemy

Water in a van freezes at 0°C. A frozen water tank means no water. Frozen pipes burst. Frozen waste water lines block.

There are two approaches: heated water systems or drain-and-refill protocols.

Heated systems: Tank heating cable (£50–100), pipe lagging (£30–50), and a small circulating pump that runs from your battery when you're active. This keeps water liquid but costs power — running a tank heater and circulation loop draws maybe 200–300W continuous. On a budget battery, that's noticeable.

Drain-down systems: When temperature drops below 5°C, you drain your fresh water tank and main pipes completely, leaving just enough water for washing hands in a kettle. Drain your gray water tank if it's sitting outside. Blowdown the waste pipe with a bicycle pump to clear standing water. It's a ritual every evening in winter, but it works reliably and costs no power.

The honest choice: drain-down if you're wild camping, heated system if you have regular mains hook-up. Combining both (drain down at night, heat during the day on hook-up) gives you flexibility.

Insulate everything. Water pipes should be wrapped in foam pipe lagging minimum — I'd use 50mm split pipe insulation on main runs. Fresh water tanks buried under insulation. Waste water tanks on the outside of the van, insulated with rigid foam blocks. This cuts freeze risk dramatically.

Windows: double glazing, not covers, if budget allows

Single-glazed windows are genuinely cold in winter. The glass is often around 2–3°C below interior temperature, which is where your condensation problem starts.

Double-glazed windows from conversion companies (VB Autoclassic, Reimo, etc.) cost £100–150 per window installed, so a full van with 4–6 windows is £600–1000. It's expensive. But it's permanent, doesn't require setup/takedown daily, and reduces heat loss by 40–50%.

Thermal covers (SilverScreens, Custom Fit Covers, etc.) are £50–100 per set and achieve similar insulating benefit. But you're fitting and removing them daily. In practice, many people install thermal covers for winter then switch to regular windows for summer visibility.

My recommendation: prioritize double glazing on windows facing prevailing wind (usually northwest in the UK). Budget-limited? Double glaze the main living area and cover the cab windows and back windows seasonally.

Practical stuff: the things they don't tell you

Bed setup: You're not staying warm by heater alone. Sleep with decent bedding — a proper duvet rated for 2–3°C comfort, not a summer sleeping bag. A flannel bed sheet makes a genuine difference. A hot water bottle in bed is cheap insurance on the coldest nights. Thermal base layers are worth more than an extra kW of heater capacity — they cost £30 and weigh nothing.

Drying wet gear: Winter means wet gear every day. A clothes airer inside the van with the fan running pulls moisture out fast. Hang everything to dry rather than storing it wet. Bring gear inside during the day if you have space — don't leave boots and wet jackets in a cold locker where they'll mold.

Cooking: Boiling a kettle once in your van is fine. Boiling it five times creates condensation chaos. Use a camping stove outside when weather allows. Keep hot drinks minimal indoors in winter.

Temperature comfort: 16°C feels cold until you're used to it. 14°C with decent clothing is sustainable. Below 12°C most people start to struggle. Set your heater thermostat to 14–16°C and expect it to be your norm, not your minimum.

The budget: what does proper winter spec cost?

Build a summer van with basic insulation, a small gas heater, 100Ah battery, and 200W solar: £8,000–12,000 in conversion costs on a used T6 platform.

Proper winter spec: add another £3,000–5,000:

  • Upgrade to closed-cell foam, thermal bridging work: +£1,500–2,000
  • Diesel heater with controls: +£1,200–1,500
  • Upgrade battery to 200Ah LiFePO4: +£1,500–2,000 (over the 100Ah)
  • Improve ventilation (MaxxFan, better ducting): +£400–600
  • Double-glazed windows or thermal covers: +£600–1,000
  • Water heating and lagging: +£300–500

Top end of that range (double glazing, 300Ah battery, premium heater): you're approaching £20,000 in conversion costs before you even buy the van. But you own a van that genuinely works year-round, rather than a van you abandon for four months.

You don't have to spec everything. Prioritize: insulation and heating first (no heat = game over). Condensation management second (wet van = rot and misery). Battery and power third. Everything else is optimization.

The honest truth

Year-round van life in the UK is brilliant. Driving Scottish coastlines in January, wild camping on the Cornish moors in February, exploring the Lake District in March — you get landscapes and experiences that most tourists never see. But it requires a properly spec'd van.

You can retrofit some of this. You can add a better heater to a poorly insulated van, or upgrade batteries later. But if you're starting from a blank slate, building a van specifically for year-round UK use, the best time to get it right is during build. Insulation and structural heating are hard to retrofit.

I've seen people try to cheap out on winter spec, thinking they'd upgrade later. Most of them either stop winter camping or end up spending double on retrofits. The van that costs £1,500 more upfront often costs £3,000 less over three years of actual use.
