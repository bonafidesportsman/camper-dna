Donor Van or Pre-Converted? The Decision That Shapes Everything

Before you choose a van type, a layout, or a single piece of kit — this is the fork in the road. Neither route is obviously better. They suit different people in different situations.

When you start hunting for a campervan, you'll be buried under choices. Roof type. Bed orientation. Diesel or petrol. Off-grid capability. But before any of that matters, you have to answer the binary question that fundamentally changes everything: do you buy a ready-made campervan, or do you buy a donor van and commission a conversion?

This choice affects your budget, your timeline, your stress levels, and ultimately whether you end up with a van you genuinely love or one that's a series of compromises.

The Pre-Converted Route: Instant Campervan Life

Walk into a dealer or marketplace and there it is — a van with a bed, a kitchen, a roof light, and a fully functioning electrical system. You can test it, poke at the cupboards, flip the shower tap. Within weeks, you're camping.

This is the appeal of pre-converted vans, and for many people it's the right choice.

The advantages

You get immediate use. There's no waiting 6, 8, or even 12 months for a converter. If you're booking a summer trip in May, a pre-built van arrives in April. No delays, no excuses, no build-updates-going-silent.

You can test before committing at scale. A two-week hire is good reconnaissance, but living in your own van for a fortnight — actually sleeping in it, cooking in it, dealing with condensation and cold — is different. You'll understand whether you actually want a rock-hard side bed or if it should be longer. Whether a kitchenette is sufficient or you need better storage.

Financing is more straightforward. Banks understand used vans. They'll lend against a T5 or T6 Transporter far more readily than against a hypothetical conversion mid-build. Your insurance company won't ask uncomfortable questions about electrical system spec or whether the work meets NCC standards.

You avoid project management. The conversion company is not your responsibility. The van is already someone else's responsibility — and their problem if the heater fails at month three.

The disadvantages

You're living in someone else's specification. That side kitchen? The previous owner thought it was essential. You'd have preferred that space for a larger fridge or more cupboards. Now you're either adapting to their layout or paying to have it all ripped out and rebuilt — which defeats the purpose of buying pre-converted.

Build quality is opaque until you own it. A conversion company has accountability and a workshop you can visit. A private sale? You're judging quality from photos and a brief walkthrough. By the time you discover that the roof light isn't properly sealed or that the electrical system was wired by a enthusiast rather than an auto-electrician, you own it.

Good pre-converted vans sell fast. The market for a well-spec'd, clean T6 conversion is intensely competitive. You're competing against 15 other buyers who've all spotted the same van on the same day.

Modifying a pre-built van is harder and more expensive. The furniture is often built-in and integrated with the shell. Want to upgrade the heater? It's probably bonded to the wall, not bolted. Want to add solar? The roof might be full, or the electrical system might not be robust enough. You're often making do rather than making it exactly right.

The Donor Van + Conversion Route: Exactly What You Want

Start with a raw VW Transporter — a used one, say a 2019 T6.1 with low mileage and a clean service history. No kitchen. No bed. Just mechanical integrity and a blank box. Hand it to a reputable conversion company and say: here's what I want. Twelve weeks later, you've got a van that's built to your exact specification.

The advantages

You get exactly what you want, not what the previous owner wanted. Your bed length, your electrical capacity, your insulation strategy. If you need a shower, you get a proper one — not a token rinse cubicle squeezed between the toilet and a cupboard. If you don't need a shower but do need a proper work desk, the converter builds around that.

You control quality. You visit the workshop. You see the base vehicle before they start. You're there when they're fitting the roof or wiring the leisure battery. You review the electrical diagram. You understand what you're buying and why. If something doesn't meet your standard, it gets fixed before you pay the final invoice.

You choose the base vehicle. A donor van route means you can be strategic about the Transporter generation, mileage, engine spec, and condition. You're not hunting for a pre-built conversion on a T5 when a T6.1 would actually suit you better — you just buy the right base vehicle.

You have a self-build option. Many converters will supply a kit and let you handle some of the non-structural work — cabinetry, upholstery, some electrical connections. This can reduce costs significantly and give you a genuine sense of ownership and understanding.

The disadvantages

Lead times are long. Even efficient converters have 12 to 20-week waiting lists depending on the month. If you're committed to a summer trip, you need to start now — not in April. The waiting period is genuinely stressful if you're a planner with a fixed timeline.

You're making decisions on drawings, not from lived experience. You've never actually slept in a side-facing bed in a narrow van. You think you know whether you want a cassette toilet or a composting one, but you're probably wrong. You're committing to layouts based on floor plans and the converter's advice, and you can't test-drive the concept. Some people get it exactly right. Many don't.

Project management is your responsibility. The conversion company will manage the build, but you need to be responsive. Decisions can't wait. Change requests need to be carefully considered — they cost money and time. The electrical system supplier goes on backorder? That's a delay you need to handle communication-wise, because it affects your van.

Cost overruns are common. A quote of £22,000 for a conversion can become £24,500 by month two. Unforeseen issues, upgraded components, change requests — they accumulate. You need contingency and flexibility, because the final invoice won't match the initial one.

Who Each Route Actually Suits

Buy pre-converted if:

  • You have a specific trip or timeline you can't move
  • You want to test campervan life without major commitment
  • You're drawn to a specific van you've found and can verify its history
  • You have limited budget and want to avoid unexpected costs
  • You're confident you can live with someone else's specification

Commit to a conversion if:

  • You have a specific use case that requires a custom layout (e.g., long-term winter van, workshop-mobile, carer van)
  • You're willing to wait 4-6 months for the right van
  • You want to understand and control the build quality completely
  • You're prepared for cost overruns and have contingency
  • You're investing significantly — over £35,000 — and want to protect that investment with exactly the right spec

The Overlooked Middle Route

Here's what most first-time buyers don't consider: buy an older, lower-spec pre-converted van — say a T5 from 2010-2014, priced between £18,000 and £22,000 — and then upgrade it incrementally.

You'll get immediate use. You can test the campervan lifestyle. But you're not married to someone else's premium specification. The electrical system is basic? Upgrade to a lithium battery and an MPPT controller over the winter for £3,000. The heater is noisy? Replace it with a proper Alde unit. The insulation is thin? You can't change that, but you can upgrade heating.

The total investment ends up similar to a brand-new conversion — £25,000 to £27,000 all-in — but you've had two years of active use, you understand your actual needs, and you've built a van that's genuinely yours.

It's not glamorous. You're not unboxing a showroom-fresh conversion. But it's often the most rational starting point.
