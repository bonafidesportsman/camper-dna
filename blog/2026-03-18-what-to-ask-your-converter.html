What to Ask a Conversion Company Before You Commit

You're about to hand someone your van and somewhere between £15,000 and £40,000. These are the questions that separate a good experience from a stressful one.

Choosing a conversion company is one of the highest-stakes decisions in the campervan journey. You're entrusting someone with your vehicle, your money, and months of build time. The company you choose will determine whether you end up with a robust, well-engineered van or a ticking time-bomb of electrical faults and water ingress.

Most people defer to portfolio and recommendation. They see pretty photos and positive reviews and think the due diligence is done. But asking the right technical questions — before you sign anything — is how you actually separate the professionals from the chancers.

Build Quality and Process

A professional converter should be confident showing you their work mid-build. If a company is vague or uncomfortable about you visiting during the conversion, that's a genuine red flag. You need to understand their actual process, not their marketing narrative.

Ask specifically: Can I visit the workshop to see a van mid-build? Not their showroom or office. The workshop. Where a van is actually being worked on. Watch them build sections of the floor, the roof, the electrical system. You'll learn more in two hours than in six months of email updates.

Push on insulation specifics. Insulation is invisible once the van is finished, so it's where cost-cutting happens. Ask: What insulation do you use? What brand, what thickness, what type? Are they using PIR boards (rigid foam, good R-value) or cheaper mineral wool? Are they using 50mm or 25mm? Insulation quality determines how warm the van is, how much condensation you'll fight, and how expensive your heating bills are going to be.

Ask about attachment methodology: How is furniture attached to the van? Is it bolted to the floor or bonded? Bonding looks neater, but bolting is more robust and repairable. If a bonded cabinet starts to come loose at the joint, you're paying for it to be reattached. A bolted cabinet, you tighten the bolts. Bolting also means future owners can modify the van more easily.

Understand their design philosophy: Do you use templates and jigs for your builds, or is every van bespoke? This sounds like a detail, but it matters enormously. A good converter has templates that they've refined over dozens of builds. They know how to maximise space, where plumbing needs to run, how to balance weight. A converter who designs everything bespoke is either a genius or inefficient. Usually inefficient, which means delays and cost overruns.

Electrical Systems and Energy

The electrical system is the most complex part of a modern campervan conversion, and it's where most problems emerge. A properly designed system is robust and fault-tolerant. A bodged one will introduce years of frustration.

First question: Who designs the electrical system? Is it the converter's in-house staff or an external auto-electrician? There's nothing wrong with outsourcing, but you want to know. And ideally, you want the auto-electrician involved from the start, not brought in at the end to wire up components that weren't designed for power requirements.

Ask about sign-off: Will the electrical system be designed and certified by a qualified auto-electrician? The answer should be yes. And their name and certification should be documented in the handover paperwork.

Battery chemistry matters more than most people realise. What battery type will you use — AGM or lithium (LiFePO4)? AGM is the old standard. It's reliable, well-understood, but less efficient and lower capacity for the same size. Lithium is the modern choice — higher usable capacity, better performance in cold weather, longer lifespan — but it needs a proper battery management system. If a converter is using cheap AGM batteries and claiming it's a premium conversion, that's a shortcut. If they're using lithium, ask about the BMS (battery management system). How good is it? How does it communicate with the charger?

Get specifics on total system capacity: What's the total amp-hour capacity of the leisure battery bank? Not vague answers about "plenty of power for a week off-grid." Give me numbers. A typical modern conversion is 200Ah lithium or 300Ah AGM. Know what you're getting.

Ask about solar: What solar panels will be fitted? What wattage and what type? 200W is minimum if you want meaningful charging. 400W is more realistic. And ask about the solar charge controller: Is it a PWM or MPPT controller? MPPT is more efficient and worth the extra cost. Get the specifications in writing.

Most importantly, request: Will I receive a complete wiring diagram and a system manual documenting all components, their specifications, and how to operate them? If the converter hasn't prepared this, they're not confident enough in their own work. A proper handover includes documentation. Everything from battery capacity to circuit breaker ratings to solar panel specifications.

Timeline and Communication

Lead times are often optimistic. Converters have waiting lists for a reason. Ask bluntly: What's your current lead time from order to handover? Not the advertised lead time. The actual current one. If it's longer than their website says, that tells you about their accuracy.

Then ask the hard follow-up: How do you handle delays? Because there will be delays. Component shortages, unexpected structural issues, their supplier letting them down. What's their communication protocol if the build is going to slip? Will you get regular updates? Will they absorb some of the delay cost, or is it entirely your problem?

Ask about access: Can I visit during the build? How frequently? And who's my single point of contact for questions? You want one person's name, not a email address that bounces between departments. Someone who knows your van inside and out and will answer your questions without delay.

Request progress updates: Will you provide photos during the build? Monthly minimum. This serves two purposes: you can catch issues early, and you can document the process for your own understanding.

Cost and Payment Structure

A clear quote is a sign of professionalism. What's included in the quoted price and what's optional extra? You need to understand the baseline, then see what upgrades cost. Is solar included? Heating? A shower? Clarify this completely before signing.

Payment structure matters enormously. A reputable converter operates on stage payments: deposit (usually 25-30% to secure the build slot), stage payments as work completes (typically at 25%, 50%, and 75% progress), and final balance on handover. Ask specifically: What's your payment schedule? When are deposits due, when are stage payments due, and when is final payment?

Is the initial deposit refundable? It shouldn't be, technically — they're allocating workshop capacity to you. But understand the terms. If you withdraw at month three, what's your financial exposure?

Ask the uncomfortable question: What happens if costs increase during the build? Get this in writing. Are there caps? Do they absorb minor overruns? Who pays if they need a more expensive component than originally quoted? Most professional converters will have a policy that they absorb small overruns (under £500) but anything significant requires customer approval and additional payment. That's reasonable. No policy at all is concerning.

Warranty and Aftercare

Warranty is where you separate professional converters from hobbyists. Ask: What warranty do you offer on the conversion as a whole? The industry standard is 2-5 years on the conversion workmanship. Make sure it's in writing and specifies what's covered.

Then ask about individual components: What warranties come with the heater, the roof, the battery system, the solar panels? These are manufacturer warranties that come with the components, but also ask if the converter offers extended warranties or guarantees on their installation of these components.

This is critical: What happens if something fails at month six? Am I covered? If the heater stops working or the solar controller fails, is it repair or replacement? Are you charged labour, or is it covered? Do you need to return to their workshop or can it be fixed elsewhere?

Ask about snagging. What's your snagging period after handover? You should have at least 30 days to identify any issues — a cabinet not closing properly, a light that doesn't work, a leak. Professional converters will rectify minor snagging items at no cost during this period. If they push back on snagging, that's a warning sign.

Compliance and Documentation

A modern campervan conversion is a serious modification. Will the van meet NCC standards or equivalent? NCC is the National Caravan Council standard — it's the British industry benchmark for conversion safety. Not all converters achieve it (it costs money and involves inspection), but it's the gold standard.

Ask: Will the van need to be re-registered with the DVLA as a motor caravan? Some conversions do, some don't, depending on the structural modifications and weight. If it does need re-registering, does the converter handle the paperwork or is that your responsibility? If it's yours, that's a hassle you weren't expecting.

Related question: Will the finished van pass a habitation check? Not all pre-registered conversions do. A habitation check is an inspection that certifies the van is legally habitable (proper ventilation, heating, plumbing). If you're planning to rent the van out or claim it as a second residence for insurance purposes, you need this. Know whether the converter will deliver it.

Most importantly: What documentation will I receive at handover? You should get the wiring diagram (we covered this), a service manual, a parts list with warranty information, and records of the work completed. If they haven't prepared this, they're not set up for professional aftercare.

Red Flags That Should End the Conversation

Some warning signs should disqualify a converter immediately. If they can't or won't let you visit the workshop, leave. If they're vague about costs and can't give you an itemised quote, leave. If they're pressuring you to pay a large deposit without stage payments, leave.

If they have no portfolio of previous work or can't provide customer references, that's a red flag. If they're reluctant to put warranty terms in writing, don't trust them. If they can't explain their electrical system design or provide component specifications, they don't know what they're building.

And if you ask these questions and they get defensive or dismissive, that tells you everything about how they'll treat you during the build when issues emerge.
