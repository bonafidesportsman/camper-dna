How much does a campervan conversion cost in 2026?

People wildly underestimate what a proper build costs — and wildly overestimate what a cheap one delivers. Here's an honest breakdown across three budget levels.

When you start researching campervan conversions, you'll stumble across the same myth repeated across forums and Facebook groups: "I did my entire conversion for £4k." This person either built it at exceptional speed with skills most people don't have, or they're not counting everything. Usually both.

The truth is messier and more useful: conversion costs depend entirely on three things — what you want, where you're starting from, and whether you're building it yourself or paying someone else. I've seen brilliant £5,000 self-builds and £25,000 professional conversions that disappointed. The difference isn't budget — it's honesty about what budget gets you.

Here's what different price points actually deliver in 2026.

Budget DIY conversion: £3,000–£8,000

This is the self-build route. You're doing all the work yourself, and you've either got welding skills, joinery skills, or you're prepared to learn fast and make mistakes. You've got a van, basic tools, and weekends free for 6–12 weeks. Realistically, you're also doing this in a garage or driveway, not in a car park.

What this actually includes:

  • Insulation: Spray foam or mineral wool in the walls and roof. About £400–600 in materials. It's messy, uneven, and you'll get overspray everywhere — but it works.
  • Bed: A DIY wooden box with a second-hand mattress or custom foam. £200–500. It'll be 1.9m long if you're lucky, 1.85m if you're not tall enough to complain.
  • Kitchen: A single portable camping stove, a coolbox or small 40-litre fridge, and a basic sink with manual pump. £300–500. You're not making espresso here.
  • Electrics: A leisure battery (100Ah lithium from a Chinese brand or a second-hand 200Ah lead-acid), a 1000W inverter, basic wiring, and a CTEK charger. £400–800. You're running a heater, lights, and fridge, not a television.
  • Heating: A diesel heater from AliExpress (Eberspächer copy) or, if you're sensible, a used Webasto. £200–500. The Chinese ones are genuine and work, but installation is fiddly. Webasto is quieter and proven.
  • Windows and vents: Two or three cheap plastic roof vents, one side window replacement. £400–600. Condensation will happen anyway.
  • Flooring: Plywood subfloor, vinyl lino, and carpet over the top. £300–400. It'll feel cheap because it is.
  • Driving and furniture: One basic swivel driver's seat, cushions, shelving from B&Q. £400–600.
  • Sundries: Wood, fixings, paint, cable, fuses, panels, and mistakes. £500–1,000. This is where the budget gets eaten.

That gets you to roughly £4,000–£5,500 in materials. But there are costs you'll forget about entirely until you hit them.

The hidden costs nobody mentions

This is where budget self-builds actually fail.

Rust remediation: You won't know your van's full rust situation until you pull trim panels off. That £200 patch of rust on the floor after removing a seat turns into two days of wire-brushing and treating. If you're unlucky — and many people are — rear corners, sliding door bottoms, and floor sections have serious corrosion. A proper respray of one panel is £200–400. A full underbody blasting and sealing is £1,000–1,500. Budget £500 minimum and expect to spend it.

Mechanics: Your van's 80,000+ miles old. The timing belt might be due, the alternator might be tired, and the glow plugs might be questionable. A timing belt is £400–600. That's on top of your conversion budget. So is new brake pads if your van needs them, which it probably does.

Windows and seals: If you're cutting a window into the van, you need a Dremel, a carbide bit, careful measurement, and a new frame/seal kit. The windows themselves are £150–300 each. If you get the template wrong — and you might — you've got a 6cm hole in your van's side and a ruined window frame to replace. Budget £50–100 extra for mistakes.

DVLA reclassification: Once you've built a conversion, you need to notify the DVLA and potentially reclassify the vehicle from van (category N1) to motorhome (category N1G). This costs £165 and requires an MOT station to inspect the conversion. If your build doesn't meet weight limits or safety standards, you'll be asked to modify it. Budget £200–300 to be safe.

Gas installation and sign-off: If you want a proper gas boiler for your heater or hob, it must be installed by a Registered Installer and certified to BS EN 1949:2000. This isn't optional if you want insurance and MOT compliance. A certified installation costs £600–1,200. Many budget self-builders skip this entirely, which is why they have insurance disputes later.

Upholstery and finishing: You budgeted £400 for cushions, but your bed actually needs 10cm depth. That's another £200. The walls look bare, so you add fabric linings. That's another £300. You wanted storage baskets instead of bare wood shelves. Another £250. These aren't mistakes — they're the difference between camping and actually living in your van.

Real budget for a DIY build with hidden costs: £6,000–£10,000. If you hit unexpected rust or mechanical issues, £12,000.

Mid-range semi-professional conversion: £10,000–£20,000

This is the route where you're either paying a specialist converter to do the heavy lifting, or you're a skilled DIYer with proper workshop space and you're taking your time to do it properly.

You're getting a better outcome because you're solving the problems budget builds ignore.

  • Insulation: Closed-cell spray foam on walls and roof, properly done, with boards over the top to protect it. £800–1,200. It's even, it's sealed, and condensation is dramatically reduced.
  • Bed: A proper wooden frame with a 200mm pocket-sprung mattress. 2.0m × 1.4m as a platform. £1,200–1,800. You can sleep diagonally if you need to, and the bed doesn't sag after a month.
  • Kitchen: A small but real hob (two-burner gas), a proper sink with hot and cold running water (fed from a 40-litre tank), a 70-litre fridge, and basic storage. £1,500–2,500. You can make coffee and cook an actual meal.
  • Electrics: A 300Ah lithium battery or quality 400Ah lead-acid, a 3000W pure sine wave inverter, 800W of solar panels, MPPT controller, proper wiring, fuses, and battery management system. £2,500–4,000. You can run your fridge all day and a heater at night without the engine running.
  • Heating: A proper Webasto diesel heater with thermostat control, or an equivalent quality unit. £800–1,200 including installation. It's quiet, reliable, and won't wake your neighbours.
  • Windows and vents: Two to three quality roof vents, two side windows, decent seals. £1,200–1,800. You can actually control condensation.
  • Flooring: Plywood subfloor with proper vapour barrier, vinyl or lino, quality carpet or rubber matting. £600–1,000. It feels solid and lasts longer than budget vinyl.
  • Furniture and finishes: Custom built-in storage, quality seat cushions, fabric lining on walls, proper reading lights, and a cassette toilet. £1,500–2,500. The van actually feels finished, not like a building site.

That's £10,000–£16,000 in materials and parts. But here's where mid-range conversions differ: you're also paying for competence. If a specialist is doing this work, add their labour — £3,000–£6,000 depending on what you're outsourcing.

A mid-range semi-professional build handles the hidden costs properly:

  • Rust is found early and fixed properly, not bodged
  • Mechanical problems are addressed before building starts
  • Gas is installed by a certified engineer
  • Electrical systems are properly sized and won't overheat or fail
  • Weight distribution is calculated and documented
  • Everything is warrantied

Real cost for a mid-range build: £13,000–£22,000 depending on how much you DIY versus outsource.

Full professional conversion: £20,000–£40,000+

This is the route where a professional converter does everything, and you hand over the keys and get a finished vehicle. You're paying for their experience, their workshop infrastructure, their relationships with suppliers, and their warranty.

What changes at this level isn't features — it's execution and reliability.

  • Insulation: Multi-layer spray foam with vapour barriers, professional finish. £1,500–2,000.
  • Bed: Custom-built wooden frame, 200mm pocket-sprung or premium memory foam mattress, with storage beneath. £2,000–3,000.
  • Kitchen: A three-burner or two-burner hob with grill, proper stainless steel or enamelled sink with hot/cold water, a 90-litre fridge, extractor fan, and integrated storage. £2,500–4,000.
  • Electrics: 400–500Ah lithium or lead-acid, 3000W inverter, 800W+ solar (often split roof panels for aesthetics), MPPT, integrated management system, uprated alternator if needed. £3,500–5,500.
  • Heating: Webasto or Eberspächer heater with proper ducting, thermostat, and hot-water system integrated with your kitchen. £1,500–2,500.
  • Windows and vents: Quality roof vents with blinds, pop-top roof window (if T6), side windows, all properly sealed. £2,000–3,500.
  • Flooring and finishes: Engineered wood or vinyl flooring with underlay, upholstered walls, custom cabinetry, quality lighting throughout (including LED strips and reading lights), cassette toilet with odour extraction. £3,000–5,000.
  • Driving experience: Custom captain's chairs or upgraded OEM seats, steering wheel controls, properly integrated audio system (often with reversing camera), finished trim throughout. £2,000–3,500.

Materials and parts: £18,000–£28,000. Labour from a professional converter: £4,000–£12,000+ depending on build complexity and their day rate.

What you actually get at this price point: a vehicle that's safe, warrantied, compliant with all regulations, and built to a standard where every seal, every electrical connection, and every gas fitting has been done by someone who's done it 50 times before. It won't have condensation problems. The fridge will work efficiently. The electrics won't be a fire hazard. The heating will warm you in January. The DVLA will approve it without question.

Real cost for a full professional conversion: £22,000–£40,000+. Premium converters working with T6.1 builds or luxury specifications can charge £45,000–£60,000. You're essentially getting a custom-built motorhome at that point.

The case for buying converted versus building yourself

There's a crossover point where buying a used professionally converted van becomes cheaper than building your own.

If you can find a three-year-old professional conversion that originally cost £30,000, you might buy it for £18,000–£22,000. You're getting all the warranty benefits already realised (no surprises), all the problems already fixed (the converter dealt with the rust issues), and you're getting a vehicle you can use immediately.

Compare that to building your own for £15,000 over 12 weeks of weekends, making mistakes you'll regret, not getting a warranty, and discovering problems three months in when you're 200 miles from home.

For most first-time buyers, a used converted van from a reputable builder — something five years old that's been properly maintained — is better value than a DIY build at the same budget.

The exception: if you've got genuine building skills (joinery, plumbing, electrics), plenty of time, and you see the build as part of the experience, a mid-range DIY conversion is the sweet spot. You'll learn your van intimately. You'll know every system. You can modify it later without fear. And you'll spend less money doing it.

The VAT trap on professional conversions

If you're buying a professionally converted campervan, check carefully whether VAT has been added to the conversion costs. A professional converter building to order should be including VAT in their quote (it's their legal responsibility), but some operate in grey areas.

On a £20,000 conversion, VAT at 20% is an extra £4,000. That's not a hidden cost if you're paying it — it's just the legal cost of working with a VAT-registered business. But some buyers have found themselves quoted £20k only to be told the final invoice is £24k once VAT is added.

Clarify this upfront. A legitimate converter will quote you the total price including VAT.

Weight and payload reality

Every van has a kerb weight and a MTPLM (Maximum Technically Permissible Laden Mass). You need to know both, because adding a conversion means adding weight.

A typical T6 has about 1,200 kg of payload available when you buy it empty. A proper conversion — bed, kitchen, insulation, electrics, fridge, water, fuel in the heater — weighs 400–600 kg. That leaves 600–800 kg for yourselves, luggage, and fuel.

Some conversions (particularly full-fat professional builds with heavy appliances) eat more payload. If you're a couple plus a dog plus 150 litres of water in summer, you're tight. Many budget builds don't even calculate this, and the first time you get weighed at a vehicle check, you discover you're overweight and technically uninsured.

Any converter worth paying should provide you with a payload calculation and document it. If they don't mention weight, don't work with them.

The honest truth about the £5,000 conversion

A £5,000 professional conversion doesn't exist in 2026. Even if someone offers you one, you're not getting a conversion — you're getting a van with some cupboards bolted in. That's not a judgement; it's maths. A professional converter's day rate is £400–600. Labour for even a basic two-week build is £4,000–5,000 before materials.

A £5,000 self-build can be brilliant if you have the skills. You can buy an older T5, fit it with everything you actually need (bed, cooker, fridge, heating, electrics), and have a perfectly usable van. The difference is you're doing the work yourself. Your labour is free (or rather, valued at your time). A professional's labour isn't.

There's no shame in either route. Just be honest about what you're comparing.

What different people actually need

Budget isn't the only factor. What you'll actually use the conversion for matters enormously.

If you're doing European trips with your partner and spending three months a year on the road: full professional conversion, £25,000–£35,000. You'll use every system extensively. Heating matters. Water system matters. Electricity matters. Comfort matters. Warranty matters.

If you're doing UK weekends and a week in summer: mid-range conversion, £12,000–£18,000. You want it comfortable and reliable, but you don't need luxury. A decent kitchen, good heating, solid bed, and enough power for your fridge. You can DIY some of this if you have skills.

If you're testing whether you'll actually use a campervan regularly: budget self-build, £6,000–£8,000. You might discover you prefer hotels. You might discover you love it and want to upgrade later. Either way, you haven't spent £25,000 on a decision. I know several people who built a £7k conversion, used it once, and sold it. No regrets.

2026 reality check

Inflation's hitting the conversion industry harder than most sectors. Insulation foams cost more. Lithium batteries cost more. Labour costs more. Compared to 2023, a professional conversion is roughly 15–20% more expensive for the same spec.

Chinese-brand heating units and electronics are cheaper than ever, which is why DIY budgets can stay reasonable. But importing also means no UK warranty, so if something fails, you're ordering a replacement from AliExpress and waiting three weeks.

Professional UK converters are suffering from material costs and supply chain delays. Many now build to order with 8–12 week lead times. That's actually better for your situation (you get exactly what you want) but harder for your patience.

If you're considering a conversion, do it soon or wait until next year. The in-between point of 2026 is where prices are highest and timelines are longest.

The real cost calculation

Whatever budget tier you choose, the real cost isn't what you spend upfront. It's what you spend maintaining a vehicle that was either rushed or neglected during building.

A budget DIY build with shortcuts (no gas certification, poor insulation, cheap electrics) might cost £7,000 upfront but £4,000 a year in repairs and problems.

A professional £30,000 build might cost £500 a year in maintenance — just the stuff you'd expect (new tyres, servicing, winter checks).

The cost difference dissolves over three years of ownership. Choose based on what you can afford upfront and what you can afford to maintain, not just what the initial number looks like.
